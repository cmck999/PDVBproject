PDVoice Buddy — how to use Not a medical device

PDVoice Buddy

A light that stays on while your voice is carrying

For tablets and phones (Android) · For computers (Windows and Mac)

What it does

Parkinson's makes voices quieter without the person noticing. You feel like you're speaking normally; the room hears a whisper.

PDVoice Buddy listens to the room and shows you one thing: a glowing dot. Bright means your voice is carrying. Dim means it's fading, and it's time to push a little.

That's the whole app. No exercises, no scores, nothing to read while you're mid-sentence.

It works out how loud the room is and asks you to rise above it — and it also asks you to reach a normal conversational level, 60–70 dB. You have to clear both. A busy café asks more of you than a quiet kitchen, but a silent room still won't accept a whisper.

Nothing is ever recorded. It measures how loud the room is, about twenty times a second, keeps that one number, and throws the sound away. There is no recording to find, because none is ever made.

The tablet version and the computer version are the same app, with the same measurement and the same settings.

It works in English and Spanish. Normally it just matches your device, so there is nothing to do — but if your device is set to English and you'd rather read Spanish, you can change it in settings. See Part 4.

Part 1 — Getting it onto a tablet or phone

PDVoice Buddy isn't in the Play Store yet, so Android will try to stop you three times while installing. None of the warnings mean anything is wrong — they appear for every app that doesn't come from the store.

  1. Tap the download link. If your browser says the file can't be scanned or may be harmful, choose Download anyway.
  2. Open the downloaded file — tap it in the notification, or find it in Files → Downloads.
  3. If Android says your browser isn't allowed to install apps, tap Settings on that message, switch on Allow from this source, then press Back.
  4. If you see a warning about an unsafe app or an unknown developer, tap More details or Install anyway, then Install.
  5. Tap Open, and allow the microphone when asked.

If you get stuck

No "Download anyway"
Pull down the notification shade and tap the download, or open Files → Downloads.
"App wasn't installed"
An older copy is already there. Uninstall PDVoice Buddy, then retry.
Can't find the setting
Settings → Apps → Special app access → Install unknown apps → choose your browser → switch on.
Play Protect blocks it
Tap More details first — the Install anyway option is hidden until you do.

Part 2 — Getting it onto a computer

There's no real installation — it's a single file.

  1. Download PDVoice Buddy.exe and put it somewhere you'll find again. Your Desktop is fine.
  2. Double-click it.
  3. Windows will probably show a blue box saying "Windows protected your PC". This is expected — it only means the app doesn't come from a big software company. Click More info, then Run anyway.
  4. Allow the microphone when asked, or the light can't work.

A small pill appears in the corner of your screen. After a second or two it's listening. To make it easier next time, open the gear and turn on Open at login — then it starts by itself and there's nothing to remember.

Part 3 — Using it

The first few seconds

When it starts it says "Listening to the room" for a second or two. That's it learning what your room sounds like with nobody talking. If you move to a different room, or the room gets much noisier, pause and unpause it so it relearns.

Just talk

Bright and glowing. They can hear you. Keep going.
Dim and small. You're fading. Speak up.
Growing and shrinking. It follows every word, so you can see yourself getting louder before you reach the target.

Don't stare at it. The dot is meant for the corner of your eye, while your attention stays on the person you're talking to.

The buttons

▮▮  Pause
Stops it listening. Press it for private calls, or when you're watching television. Press again — it becomes a triangle — to start listening again.
⚙  Gear
Settings.
✕  Close
Tablet: closes the app. Computer: hides it, still listening — the icon stays near the clock, click that to bring it back. To stop it completely, use Quit in settings.

On the full-screen size you can also tap or click anywhere to open settings. On a computer, Esc hides it.

Part 4 — Settings

Seven settings, and you'll probably only ever change the first. Microphone calibration is the one to leave alone unless a therapist asks you to. (Where to put the device below isn't a setting — it's placement advice, and it's worth reading.)

How loud you need to be

Five choices. Normal conversation sits between 60 and 70 dB, and that's what they're built around.

Gentle
about 57 dB
just below conversational, for starting out
Easy
about 60 dB
a relaxed voice
Normal
about 63 dB
conversational — where most people should be
Firm
about 66 dB
projecting deliberately
Strong
about 69 dB
strong projection

The dot needs your voice to clear two bars at once: above the room, and above the level in that list. In a noisy restaurant the room is what's holding you back. In a silent living room the level is — which is the point, because being louder than a silent room proves nothing.

Pick one you can already manage.

If the dot is dark most of the time, the setting is too high — go down a step. You want a dot that's lit more often than not.

If you're working with a speech therapist, ask them where to set it, and raise it a step every week or two rather than all at once.

Where to put the device

About an arm's length away — roughly three feet, or a metre.

A microphone hears a quieter sound the further away it is, so a tablet across the room will think you're too quiet when you aren't, and one right under your chin will think you're fine when you're not. On the table in front of you is right; on a shelf across the room is not.

Colour

Mint, amber, sky or white. Whichever you notice most easily.

Size

Full screen — a large dot in the middle. Easiest to see across a room. On a tablet this can also run as the screensaver, so a docked tablet becomes a permanent desk light with nothing to launch.

Small pill — a narrow bar that floats on top of whatever else you're doing, so you can use the device normally. Drag it wherever you like; it remembers. It has its own pause, gear and ✕ buttons.

On a tablet, the first time you choose Small pill, Android will send you to a settings screen to allow it. Allow it, then come back — the pill will be running.

Microphone calibration (leave this alone)

Every device's microphone is a little different, and none of them are real sound level meters. This setting is the app's guess at how to turn what the microphone hears into a dB number. It shows the current reading and lets you nudge it a dB at a time.

It's here for a therapist with a proper meter — or a free meter app — who wants the numbers to line up. If the dot behaves sensibly for you, there's nothing to do here. Press Reset if you change it and regret it.

Language

English or Spanish. Automatic means it follows whatever your phone, tablet or computer is set to — that's how it arrives, and for most people it's already correct.

Change it if the device is set to one language but you'd rather read the other, which is common when somebody else set the device up for you. The choices are shown in their own language, so Español reads as Español no matter which language the app is currently in.

The change happens immediately. Nothing restarts, and nothing else is affected.

Open at login (computer only)

Starts it automatically when you turn the computer on. Recommended.

In the car (tablet and phone only)

A device cannot tell whether you're driving or in the passenger seat, so it has to ask.

Not in a car
Normal.
I'm a passenger
The dot stays on. Nobody is steering.
I'm driving
The screen goes dark and you get a short buzz when your voice drops. Nothing to look at.

Important

This resets every time you open the app. If you're going to drive, set it to I'm driving each time. A moving light in a driver's line of sight is dangerous and against the law in many places.

Part 5 — Leaving it running

Tablet or phone. While it's listening there's a notice in the top bar. That's Android's rule, not a choice — any app using the microphone has to show one. Tap it to come back to PDVoice Buddy. To stop it completely: the ✕ on the pill, or close the app.

Computer. It's meant to be left on. Hiding it with ✕ keeps it listening. The icon near the clock — on Windows you may need to click the small arrow to see hidden icons — shows it's running. Click it to show or hide, right-click for Settings and Quit.

Part 6 — If something's wrong

The dot never lights up, even when I speak up

First check where the device is — an arm's length away, not across the room. If it's still dark, the setting is probably too high; go down a step or two.

The dot lights up too easily

Check the device isn't right under your chin. If it's placed sensibly and still too easy, go up a step.

The dot is always lit, even when I whisper

Something in the room is noisy — a television, a fan, a dishwasher, or the fan inside the computer itself. Turn it off if you can, then pause and unpause so it relearns the room.

It says "Listening to the room" and stays that way

Give it two seconds. If it doesn't change, pause and unpause.

It says "No microphone", or the dot doesn't react at all

Check the microphone is allowed:

Android
Settings → Apps → PDVoice Buddy → Permissions → Microphone
Windows
Settings → Privacy & security → Microphone → allow desktop apps
Mac
System Settings → Privacy & Security → Microphone

On a computer, if you have several microphones — a webcam, a headset, a docking station — it may be listening through one facing away from you. Change the default input device in your sound settings.

I chose Small pill on the tablet and nothing happened

Android needs permission for a floating pill. Tap Small pill again and it'll take you to the right screen.

I can't find the pill

Tablet: open the app from its icon, switch to Full screen, then back to Small pill. Computer: look for the icon near the clock, behind the arrow for hidden icons.

One honest note

This is a homemade tool, not a medical device.

It's good at telling louder from quieter in one place, and at nudging you toward a conversational level. The dB numbers are approximations from an uncalibrated microphone, not measurements — they will not match a real sound level meter, and no decision about treatment should rest on them.

Use it as a reminder. Set the level with your speech-language pathologist rather than guessing, especially if you're already doing speech therapy.

If the dot helps you stay audible, that's the whole point. If it turns into one more thing nagging at you, close it and say so — it can be changed.
