An ambient loudness cue for Parkinson's speech. It listens to the room — not your mouth — and holds one steady light for as long as your voice is carrying. As a tabletop puck, or as software on a screen you're already looking at.

03  /  Two bodies, one cue
Build the software version first.

A desktop app costs nothing to make, needs no soldering, and lets you test whether the cue actually changes how someone speaks. If it works, the puck becomes worth building — because it works away from the desk.

Desktop app — menu-bar dot plus an always-on-top pill

The pill floats over everything, corner-parked and draggable; the menu-bar dot is the minimal mode for people who find the pill distracting. Best for video calls and desk work — exactly where hypophonia gets called out most, because the other person can't lip-read or lean in.

Tabletop puck — for the kitchen table

Meals and visits are where projection matters most, and no screen belongs there. The puck is also the only version that works for someone who doesn't use a computer — and the only one both people at the table can see.
